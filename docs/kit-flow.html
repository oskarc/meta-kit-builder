Kit Flow

You build software with an AI agent. The kit is the set of habits, records and small checks that keep that work honest — and that slowly write down the standards you already carry in your head. This page walks through it from the start.

Who is in the room

Four kinds of thing, and only one of them is a person.

02

One feature, start to finish

You ask for something. Here is everything that happens before, during and after, in order.

  1. You ask. "Add an endpoint that does X."
  2. The agent names what kind of moment this is. It has a short map of situations — a build request, a design question, a correction, a review — and says which one it is in. That decides what it reads before acting.
  3. The agent writes a contract, in four parts, before any code. The story (what you'll get, in plain words); the use cases (each thing the system must do); the guardrails (the technical rules the build must keep); and — later — the acceptance tests. It also checks whether a precedent exists: a situation like this that you decided before.
  4. You say what would disappoint you. For each guardrail: "what result would make you say this wasn't met?" One line each. "Same as it says" is a fine answer. The agent turns your lines into tests it can run.
  5. The agent asks the pre-mortem. "It's some weeks on. This shipped and turned out to be a mistake — what went wrong?" Your answer is written down. It is the cheapest way to hear the objection before anyone commits.
  6. One test is shown failing. The agent deliberately breaks one guardrail, shows you a named test go red, and puts it back. A test that has never failed has never proved anything.
  7. You approve. Now the contract goes into the log, exactly as approved. It never gets rewritten afterwards; changes get added beside it, dated.
  8. The agent builds. If it must depart from the contract, it stops and asks. It records what the work cost.
  9. The agent writes down what it learned — and does not show you. Those notes go to a ledger and wait. Nothing reaches you until something other than the agent has looked at it.
  10. The helpers run, one per turn. The auditor reads the transcript from outside. The verifier runs the tests first, then reads the rest, and writes what held and what didn't. The consolidator sorts the learning notes into candidates.
  11. Things reach you in a review batch — see section 04.
03

When you say "no, not like that"

A correction is the most valuable thing you can give the kit. It is where a standard you carry in your head becomes visible.

  1. Your words are written down exactly. What the agent offered, what you said, and any reason you gave. No paraphrase.
  2. The agent asks three short questions, once. What did you notice that made this wrong? What would have been right here? Where have you seen this before? It does not ask "why" — people cannot reliably say why they decided, and what they say when asked is not what moved them. The three questions are about the specific moment, and those answers are what can be turned into a rule.
  3. A clerk turns it into a precedent. A precedent is a decided case: the facts that made it this situation, the question, what you chose, your reasons in your words. Later contracts read the precedents that match their situation before drawing their plan.
  4. Where the standard can be tested, the clerk writes a check. A small script that fails when the standard is broken — "every log line carries the request id", "errors are caught at the boundary, not swallowed". From then on the agent cannot forget it, because something fails if it does.
04

The review batch

Every so often, things that have gathered evidence are put to you together. This is the only way a learning becomes a rule.

  1. An assembler collects what is due. Candidate learnings that something other than the agent has confirmed; proposed changes to the map; cases waiting for your ranking; disagreements between precedents; drift incidents awaiting your say. All real. Nothing is planted.
  2. You see each item statement first. Not the recommendation, not the evidence — just the claim. You give a one-line read.
  3. Then one question: "Assume this is wrong. Why?" One-sided on purpose. Asking for reasons against improves judgement; asking for reasons for and against does nothing measurable.
  4. Then the rest — the proposed decision, the evidence, any sentence in an existing skill this would contradict, quoted exactly — and you decide: try it, adopt it, caution against it, decline, hold, or reword it.
  5. From the fourth batch on, one or two items may be ones you already decided, shown again as if new. Afterwards you see your earlier decision next to today's. Nobody scores it; it is a record you can read.
  6. Decisions are written into the skills at the exact sentence, never by rewriting the whole file.
05

How your standards get written

Put the three loops together and the standard accumulates on its own. Corrections become precedents and checks. Confirmed learnings become sentences in skills. The map — the short list of situations and what to read in each — gets entries added when something was missed and removed when nothing fires. And a check that stops needing you is one that can travel to the next project, and later to someone who is not a developer.

06

What you are actually asked to do

Your touchpoints — and nothing else

At a contract: say, per guardrail, what would disappoint you; answer "this shipped and failed — why?"; watch one test go red; approve.

When you correct: say it however you say it; then answer three short questions once.

At a review: give a one-line read of each statement; answer "assume it's wrong — why?"; decide.

Never: invoke a helper, run a script, read a score. The hooks run the lifecycle; nothing measures you.

07

What changed on 13 September, and why

The kit ran a review of itself and a round of research into how software testing and the psychology of judgement solved these problems long ago. Seven things changed. Each pair below shows the old flow on the left and the new one on the right, in plain terms.

The contract gate — who writes the tests

WhyEvery method that puts acceptance tests before the code — going back to the 1990s — has the customer, not the programmer, say what "done" means. When the same mind writes the code and its tests, the tests pass while the work is wrong. And a test that has never been seen to fail has never told anyone anything.

Verification — who checks, and how

WhyAviation and automotive standards settled long ago what "independent verification" means: the checker may not be the builder, and the checker chooses what to check. The reason is one sentence: a single misreading of the spec must not reach both the thing and its check. The kit now uses that definition, and admits where its checker is the builder's own kind.

Corrections — what gets asked

WhySee section 03. Asking what was noticed in a specific case recovers what asking "why" cannot; and a standard that exists as a check is enforced rather than remembered.

The review batch — no more planted items, no more scores

WhyThe score on your decisions could be maximised by declining everything, because declining a candidate created the outcome it was then graded against. The catch rate pooled items of different difficulty at batch sizes where it could not be read. Hand-made fakes are known not to stand in for real problems, so the kit shows you your own real past decisions instead. And the research on feedback says: show the person the correct answer, never a score of themselves.

How a learning earns its place

Why"Independent" used to mean "a different helper reported it". Now it means "a single misreading of the contract could not have produced both". The formula over the old counts was noise dressed as a threshold; the counts themselves you can read.

The rebuild — taken out until there is something to rebuild against

WhyVersions built separately from the same spec fail on the same inputs — shown for human teams in 1986 and for coding agents in 2026. One rebuild compared to one original proves little; three built and voted still helps. And you cannot hold the tests out until a standard exists as tests.

08

Glossary

Pioneer
You. The human whose judgement the kit draws out. Named that way because the end result is not known at the start.
Agent
The AI that writes contracts and builds. It never grades its own work.
Helper agents
Separate AI runs that check, sort, clerk and assemble — each allowed to see only what it must.
Contract
The written plan for one feature, approved before code: story, use cases, guardrails, tests. Frozen at approval; changes are added beside it, dated.
Guardrail
A technical rule the build must keep. Each has an id (G-1, G-2…).
Disappointment line
Your answer, per guardrail, to "what result would make you say this wasn't met?" The tests are derived from it.
Pre-mortem
"It's weeks on and this failed — why?" Asked before approval, recorded verbatim.
Red test
One guardrail deliberately broken so a named test is seen to fail, then restored. Proof the tests can fail.
Verifier
The helper that runs the tests, then reads the rest, and writes what held. It chooses what to read; its report is not routed through the agent.
Ledger
Where the agent's learning notes wait, unseen, until something else has confirmed them.
Candidate
A learning note that has been sorted and is gathering evidence. It reaches you when a different reading has seen it.
Different reading
A sighting that could not share the agent's misreading of the contract: a check, the verifier, a contract where the idea wasn't loaded, or you.
Review batch
The items put to you together: candidates, map changes, cases to rank, precedent disagreements, drift to resolve — and, later, some you already decided.
Re-presented item
A real item you already decided, shown again as if new. Afterwards, then-and-now side by side. Never scored.
Correction
Any time you redirect, decline, override or reword. Recorded word for word, then three questions.
Precedent
A decided case: facts, question, what you chose, your reasons. Read before later contracts in the same kind of situation.
Check
A small script that fails when a standard is broken. Where a precedent can be tested, it becomes one.
Skill
A written standard the agent works from. The skills, together with the checks, are the organisation's standard — this is what the kit is.
Map
A short list of situations and what to read in each. The agent names the situation before acting.
Stop-gate
The hook that, at the end of each turn, hands the agent at most one kit task — audit, verify, sort, clerk, assemble — so you never have to ask.
